Successive maximization. Let p and d be integers with 1 ≤ d ≤ p, and let 𝒰 and 𝒰̂ be d-dimensional subspaces of ℝᵖ. Write xᵀy for the Euclidean inner product, T for transpose, and ‖x‖₂ = √(xᵀx) for the norm.

At step i ∈ {1, …, d}, the earlier pairs (aⱼ, âⱼ), 1 ≤ j < i, have already been chosen. Let Fᵢ be the set of pairs (a, â) with a ∈ 𝒰, â ∈ 𝒰̂, unit norms, and the following orthogonality constraints:

aTaj=0,a^Ta^j=0(j<i).

Choose (aᵢ, âᵢ) ∈ Fᵢ attaining the maximum, and define

σi=max(a,a^)∈FiaTa^=aiTa^i, θi=arccos(σi).

For i = 1, there are no earlier vectors. For every later i, each new vector must be perpendicular to the earlier vectors in its own subspace. Each remaining subspace has dimension d − i + 1 ≥ 1, so Fᵢ is nonempty and compact; the continuous inner product attains its maximum. The chosen pairs are principal vectors; their angles are principal angles.

Why the angles increase. The constraints accumulate, so Fᵢ₊₁ ⊆ Fᵢ and σᵢ₊₁ ≤ σᵢ. Unit vectors have inner product at most 1. If a feasible pair has a negative inner product, reversing either vector preserves every constraint and makes the inner product positive. Consequently,

1≥σ1≥⋯≥σd≥0, 0≤θ1≤⋯≤θd≤π2.

The displayed example. The two subspaces have dimension d = 2 in ℝ⁴. Their bases are (e₁, e₂) and (b₁, b₂), as defined above the controls. With a = cos α e₁ + sin α e₂ and â = cos β b₁ + sin β b₂, the inner product in the first example is

aTa^=cos(20°)cosαcosβ+cos(60°)sinαsinβ.

This is at most cos(20°), attained by (a₁, â₁) = (e₁, b₁). Fixing that pair leaves a = ±e₂ and â = ±b₂: only two unit directions in each subspace. Matching signs give σ₂ = cos(60°) and θ₂ = 60°. Reversing both vectors in a pair gives another maximizing pair.

A shared direction. In the second example, b₁ = e₁, so the first pair has σ₁ = 1 and θ₁ = 0. In general, for unit vectors, ‖aᵢ − âᵢ‖₂² = 2 − 2σᵢ; therefore θᵢ = 0 implies aᵢ = âᵢ.

If V and V̂ are orthonormal basis matrices for 𝒰 and 𝒰̂, these σᵢ are the singular values of V̂ᵀV. See the principal vectors and SVD visualization for that computation. Repeated singular values can make the maximizing vectors nonunique; the ordered principal angles remain well-defined.
