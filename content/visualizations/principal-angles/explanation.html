Principal vectors compare two subspaces. First choose one unit vector from each subspace to maximize their inner product. For each later pair, require each vector to be perpendicular to the vectors already chosen in its own subspace. The resulting angles are ordered from smallest to largest.

Let V and V̂ be p-by-d matrices with orthonormal columns. Let L and R be orthogonal matrices in the singular value decomposition

V^TV=Ldiag(σ1,…,σd)RT. 1≥σ1≥…≥σd≥0.

Writing ℓᵢ and rᵢ for the columns of L and R, respectively, the principal vectors and angles (0 ≤ θ₁ ≤ ⋯ ≤ θd ≤ π/2) satisfy

ui=Vri, u^i=V^ℓi, cosθi=σi.

Each set is orthonormal, and the cross inner products are zero except within a pair: uᵢᵀûⱼ = σᵢ when i = j, and 0 otherwise. Thus, for the orthogonal projector P = VVᵀ,

Pu^i=VVTV^ℓi, Pu^i=σiVri=cosθiui.

In this example: let e₁, e₂, e₃ be the standard unit vectors and choose 0° ≤ φ ≤ 90°. Set h = cos φ e₂ + sin φ e₃. One principal pair is (e₁, e₁), with angle 0°. The other is (e₂, h), with angle φ and projection Ph = cos φ e₂.

Why the input columns need not be principal vectors: define the two-dimensional rotation matrix Q(t) = [[cos t, −sin t], [sin t, cos t]]. The basis sliders use V = [e₁, e₂]Q(α) and V̂ = [e₁, h]Q(β). Then V̂ᵀV = Q(β)ᵀ diag(1, cos φ)Q(α). The displayed SVD uses L = Q(β)ᵀ and R = Q(α)ᵀ, so V̂L = [e₁, h] and VR = [e₁, e₂]. Changing α or β changes the basis columns and their coordinates; it leaves these principal vectors unchanged.

At φ = 0°, the planes coincide, both principal angles are zero, and the principal vectors are not unique. This visualization keeps e₁ and e₂ as one valid choice. For φ outside 0°–90°, the second principal angle is arccos|cos φ|, rather than φ in general.
